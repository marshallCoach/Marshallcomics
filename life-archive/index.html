The Medium Arrives

The catalogue puts a date on something no document in this archive records. A collection of ten thousand comics carries an entry point inside it, and this one has been read twice from two directions: a reader described as having loved the medium for “nearly fifty years,” which places first contact around 1977 to 1979; and a shelf whose earliest strata are Bronze Age rather than Silver, which places the beginning of deliberate collecting in the early 1980s.

Taken together the two readings describe an ordinary and very specific childhood sequence. Comics appear first as something handed to a small boy — around four or five, in Harlesden. They become something he chooses, and pays for, and goes looking for, somewhere around eight. Nothing in the collection predates his own memory of it. He did not inherit this. He started it.

The Newsagent Years

A British childhood in the early eighties did not meet comics in a comic shop. It met them on a newsagent's shelf, in weekly black-and-white Marvel UK reprints sold beside the sweets, on cheap paper, out of sequence, and priced for pocket money. The direct market barely existed outside central London. What a boy in Harlesden could reach was whatever the local shop happened to rack that week.

That constraint is legible in the collection forty years later. It is the reason the shelf begins in the Bronze-to-Copper transition rather than the Silver Age: not preference, but access. He was buying what a child in northwest London could actually buy.

The Brixton uprising in 1981 and the Falklands conflict in 1982 dominate the decade's early years. Channel 4 launches in 1982, widening what a London household watches at home.

The Year the Medium Grew Up

He turned twelve in the August of the single most consequential year in modern comics. Watchmen began in September 1986. The Dark Knight Returns had run that spring. Between them they established that the form could carry adult weight — and they did it at precisely the age when a reader stops being handed books and starts forming a taste.

It is difficult to overstate how cleanly this lines up with everything Chapter Six later finds in the data. The collection of a man who follows writers rather than characters, who buys publishing eras whole, and who reads Vertigo in collected editions, was formed by a twelve-year-old encountering Moore and Miller in the last year before the family left Harlesden.

Chapter Six

The Collection as Biography

Ten thousand, four hundred and eighty-two comics across sixty-eight boxes. Catalogued, indexed, and cross-referenced against the life that bought them. What follows is not a record of what was acquired. It is what the acquisition reveals.

10,482

Comics catalogued

The oldest book in the collection was printed in 1947 — World's Finest number twenty-six. The newest was printed this year. That is a seventy-nine year span held in one house, in one set of boxes, by one reader who has never stopped.

A collection of this size stops being a set of objects and becomes a dataset. And a dataset of purchasing decisions made continuously across three decades and three countries is, whether or not it was ever intended to be, an autobiography written in monthly instalments.

Signal One

He Follows Writers, Not Characters

This is the most revealing pattern in the dataset. A character collector abandons Daredevil when the creative team changes. A writer collector follows Brubaker from Captain America to Criminal to Winter Soldier to Fatale — across characters, across publishers, across decades.

Five hundred and twenty-nine Bendis issues is not appreciation. Read one a day and it takes a year and a half to finish. That is fifteen years of handing one writer the comics budget and trusting him with it.

Signal Two

The Geographic Fingerprint

The panel attempted to reconstruct where this collector had lived using only the books — price stickers, distribution gaps, and which publishers vanish in which decades. Tested against the record in Chapters One through Five, they were half right. The half they got wrong is the more interesting half.

Confirmed
The UK Price Stickers

“Sixteen books with UK price stickers, concentrated almost entirely in 2004 through 2006. This tells us Roberto lived in the UK for approximately two to three years in the mid-two-thousands.”

The record agrees, precisely. Brunswick Terrace, Brighton in January 2004. Marion Way, Harlesden by the autumn. Anlaby House, Shoreditch in 2005. The stickers open and close exactly on the return to Britain — and they are on Batman, Fantastic Four and Captain America. The newsagent changed. The habit did not.

Corrected
The Image Founding, 1992

“He was buying Image in America in nineteen ninety-two. Lost access to Image when he moved internationally.”

He was in London. In 1992 he was eighteen, living in South Kenton and heading to the University of Greenwich. WildCATs and Savage Dragon were bought in Britain, at British shops. The first American address in this archive is 16th Street, Manhattan — and it is dated 2012, twenty years later.

Corrected
The Nineties Gap

“One hundred and thirty-one comics from 1990 through 1997… The simplest explanation: he moved.”

The simplest explanation is age. Across that gap he was sixteen to twenty-three — school, then university, then a five-year relationship, and no disposable income. He did not lose access to comics. He briefly could not afford them. The Amsterdam move that the panel reached for does not begin until 1998, after the gap has already closed.

Image disappears from the collection for twenty years and returns, in earnest, in 2013. The archive dates the Manhattan move to 2012. Signal Four

What Is Missing

A collection is defined as much by its holes as its holdings. Four absences stand out — and each one explains something the presences cannot.

“Roberto separates what he collects from what he reads.”

Rick — S2E12

That is the resolution to the Vertigo puzzle. Preacher, Transmetropolitan and Y: The Last Man were all read — as collected editions. The boxes hold monthlies, floppies and first prints. Two distinct relationships with the same medium, running in parallel in the same person.

  • Giant-Size X-Men #1 (1975) Claremont and Cockrum; the relaunch that introduced Storm, Colossus, Nightcrawler and Wolverine. Confirmed absent — conspicuous in a collection holding the Claremont, Morrison and Krakoa eras in depth.
  • The Silver Age The 1960s are thinly represented. The earliest graded piece is Thor #169 at CGC 8.0. The entry point was the Bronze-to-Copper transition — which places the start of serious collecting in the 1980s.
  • Vertigo — only three books No Preacher, no Transmetropolitan, no Y: The Last Man, no Hellblazer. Not a gap in taste. A gap in format: those were read in trade.
  • New Mutants #98 — location unknown First Deadpool. Believed present, not yet found. Roughly $800 raw. The panel has nominated Box 39, Box 25, Box 26, and the unaudited ALL_BOXES import in turn. It remains, at time of writing, the open question of the entire project.

Signal Five

He Called It A Collection Because He Loves It

Thirty years of buying comics he wanted, with no thesis and no exit strategy, produced something that behaves like a portfolio. The returns are a by-product of taste and patience — which is the only way this outcome ever actually happens.

The counter-intuitive result is that the keys are not where the money is. The keys cost roughly eleven thousand to acquire and return sixteen and a half — a five thousand dollar gain. The eight thousand two hundred and seventy-four books that are not keys return thirty-three thousand, because a complete thirty-eight issue Nightwing run is not thirty-eight books at four dollars. It is a finished object someone else would need three years to assemble.

This is the same instinct that shows up in Chapter Five. The man who built complete runs because complete runs felt satisfying is the man who founded Marshall Comics Pressing LLC, and the man whose executive practice is built on making systems whole. The collection was the first organisational design project. It simply took thirty years and nobody commissioned it.
